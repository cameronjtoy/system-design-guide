## What happened

On May 12, 2021, Fastly deployed a software release to its edge network. It contained a bug that only appeared under a specific set of conditions, so nothing went wrong at the time.

On June 8 at 09:47 UTC, a customer pushed a **valid** configuration change, exactly the kind of change customers make all the time. It happened to meet the conditions for the bug, and **85% of Fastly's network began returning errors**. Because Fastly serves as a CDN for many large sites, much of the web appeared to go down at once.

## Response

- Fastly detected the disruption **within one minute** through its monitoring.
- Engineers identified the triggering configuration and disabled it.
- By 10:36 UTC, 49 minutes after the start, 95% of the network was operating normally.
- A permanent fix for the underlying bug began rolling out later that day.

Fastly's head of engineering wrote that the bug "should have been anticipated" and that the company would look at why testing hadn't caught it.

## Root cause

The root cause was a software defect, not the customer's change. The change was a legitimate use of the product; the bug in Fastly's code turned it into a global failure. Because customer configurations are pushed to every edge location quickly, one customer's settings could reach the code path everywhere at once.

## What changed

- Deployed the bug fix across the network.
- Committed to reviewing why the bug wasn't caught in testing, and to improving its deployment and testing processes.
- Looked at ways to limit how far a single configuration can affect the network, and to speed up recovery.

## Lessons

<Callout type="tip" title="Key takeaways">
- **Latent bugs hide behind rare inputs.** A release can look healthy for weeks until an unusual but valid input arrives. Fuzzing and testing with real-world configurations help.
- **Customer input is a deploy path.** When users can change behaviour on your shared infrastructure, their changes need the same blast-radius limits as your own.
- **Detection speed sets the floor on outage length.** One minute to detect and a quick disable kept this to under an hour.
- **Shared infrastructure concentrates risk.** Thousands of companies depending on one provider means one bug can take down a large part of the web at once.
</Callout>
