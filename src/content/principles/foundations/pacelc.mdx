## The problem it solves

CAP tells you what happens during a network partition. But most of the time the network is fine, and you still have to decide something on every single request: **wait for replicas to agree, or answer now from whichever copy is closest?** That everyday decision affects users far more often than partitions do.

## How it works

Daniel Abadi proposed PACELC in 2012:

> If there is a **P**artition, choose between **A**vailability and **C**onsistency;
> **E**lse, choose between **L**atency and **C**onsistency.

The "else" half exists because strong consistency requires coordination. A write has to reach a majority of replicas, possibly in other zones or regions, before it's acknowledged. That round trip costs latency on every request, partition or not.

| System | During partition | Normal operation | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dynamo, Cassandra, Riak (default settings) | PA | EL | Answer from any replica; repair later |
| BigTable, HBase | PC | EC | Single owner per row range |
| VoltDB / H-Store | PC | EC | Serializable transactions |
| Yahoo PNUTS | PC | EL | Per-record master, but reads may be stale |
| MongoDB (as described in 2012) | PA | EC | Single primary; failover can lose acknowledged writes |
| Google Spanner | PC | EC | Paxos groups plus TrueTime; pays latency for global consistency |

Many modern databases make this a **per-request knob**. In Cassandra and DynamoDB you choose a consistency level per read or write, trading latency against freshness for that call.

## Trade-offs

- **EL (latency-first)** gives fast, local reads and writes, but clients may read stale data and must tolerate it.
- **EC (consistency-first)** gives simpler application logic, but every write pays cross-replica coordination, and cross-region writes may add 50–150 ms.
- **Geography amplifies the choice.** Within one datacenter, strong consistency is cheap. Across continents, the speed of light makes it expensive.

## In the real world

<Callout type="example" title="DynamoDB: eventual by default, strong on request">
DynamoDB reads are eventually consistent by default, which is cheaper and lower latency. Callers can request a strongly consistent read for a specific call, which costs twice the read capacity and always goes to the leader replica. Most of a typical application's reads can tolerate slight staleness; the few that can't (for example, reading back a balance right after updating it) opt in.
</Callout>

Abadi's paper argues that the latency/consistency trade-off, not the partition case, is what really drove the design of systems like Dynamo, Cassandra and PNUTS. Their designers relaxed consistency mainly to keep latency low during normal operation.

## Interview angle

<Callout type="interview">
After you talk about CAP, add: "And when there's no partition, we still trade latency for consistency. For the timeline we'll read from the nearest replica and accept a few seconds of staleness; for account settings we'll read from the leader." That's the PACELC idea, applied per feature.
</Callout>

- Assuming strong consistency is free when the network is healthy.
- Forgetting that cross-region synchronous replication puts a round trip on every write.
