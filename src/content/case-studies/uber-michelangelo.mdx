## The problem

Uber uses machine learning across its products: estimating arrival times, matching riders and drivers, pricing, fraud detection and food delivery time predictions. Before 2016, there was no standard way to build and run these models.

Uber's 2017 post describes the situation: data scientists used many different tools; there was no reliable, standard way to build training datasets or store experiments; and engineering teams built separate, one-off systems to put each model into production. Models larger than what fit on a data scientist's desktop were hard to train at all.

## The design

Michelangelo provided machine learning as an internal service, covering the full workflow:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    D[Manage data: feature store] --> T[Train models: distributed]
    T --> E[Evaluate: compare experiments]
    E --> P[Deploy: online or batch]
    P --> S[Serve predictions]
    S --> M[Monitor: prediction vs outcome]
    M --> D
```

### Managing data and a shared feature store

Features, the inputs models learn from, are often the hardest part. Michelangelo let teams compute features with batch jobs (from the data lake) and with streaming jobs (from Kafka) for near-real-time values, then store them in a shared **feature store**. Features built by one team, such as a restaurant's average meal preparation time, could be reused by others, and the same feature definitions were used for training and serving, avoiding mismatches between the two.

### Training and evaluation

Teams trained models at scale on Uber's compute clusters, with each training run's configuration, data, metrics and resulting model recorded, so experiments could be compared and reproduced.

### Deployment and serving

Models could be deployed for **offline** batch predictions or as **online** prediction services behind an API. Online serving looked up the latest feature values from a low-latency store (Cassandra in the original design), giving predictions within milliseconds.

### Monitoring

Michelangelo logged predictions and later joined them with actual outcomes (did the trip really take that long?), so teams could track accuracy over time and detect drift.

## What to take away

<Callout type="tip" title="Key takeaways">
- **Platforms beat one-off pipelines** once many teams do the same kind of work.
- **The feature store is the heart of ML infrastructure:** consistent features for training and serving, reusable across teams.
- **Treat models like deployable services,** with versioning, staged rollout and monitoring.
- **Close the loop:** comparing predictions with real outcomes is how you notice when a model stops working.
</Callout>
